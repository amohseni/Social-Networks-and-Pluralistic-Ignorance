Homophilous Rewiring Lab

Agents start on a preferential-attachment network. Each holds one or two fixed attitudes in [0, 1]. Every sweep, each agent in random order cuts its tie to the neighbor farthest from it in attitude space and adds a tie to a uniformly random non-neighbor.

Model and measures
  • Start. Barabási–Albert graph: a clique of m+1 agents, then each newcomer ties to m agents chosen with probability proportional to degree. Mean degree is about 2m and stays fixed, since every sweep cuts N ties and adds N.
  • Rewiring. Each sweep visits agents in random order. An agent cuts its tie to the farthest neighbor (Euclidean distance in attitude space; ties broken at random), then ties to a uniformly random agent other than itself, its neighbors and the one just cut. An agent left with no ties (because others cut it earlier in the sweep) skips its turn; letting it add a tie anyway would make the tie count grow without bound.
  • Influence μ. Off by default. When on, after each sweep every agent moves a fraction μ toward its neighbors' mean attitude.
  • Established ties. All measures ignore the ties added in the latest sweep. Those are random by construction (about N of them at any moment) and most are cut in the next sweep. Degree counts all ties.
  • Assortativity r. Pearson correlation of attitudes across the two ends of each tie, averaged over attitudes. 0 means ties ignore attitude; 1 means perfect sorting.
  • Tie length. Mean attitude distance across ties divided by the mean distance between random pairs. 1 means no homophily.
  • Clusters. Connected components of durable ties (ties that have survived more than 3 sweeps) with at least 5 agents. Agents outside them are grey in cluster view.
  • Modularity Q. Newman modularity of the cluster partition over established ties. Near 0 when everyone sits in one cluster; near 1 − 1/k for k equal, fully separated clusters.

What to expect. With fixed attitudes the rule sorts ties but moves no one, so the network comes to mirror the attitude distribution. Uniform or unimodal attitudes give one long chain (one attitude) or sheet (two attitudes): ties are short in attitude space, but there are no separate groups. Bimodal attitudes with one attitude give two clusters, one per mode, joined only by ties that last a sweep or two.

Two uncorrelated bimodal attitudes give four groups of agents but usually only one to three clusters. An agent cuts the tie that disagrees on both attitudes before one that disagrees on only one, so ties across a single divide survive and bridge groups. Raising the correlation toward 1 merges the four groups into two and the bridges disappear.

Preferential-attachment hubs dissolve within about 50 sweeps, since new ties go to uniformly random agents. A milder inequality replaces them: agents with typical attitudes (near the center of a mode) gather ties, while agents with extreme attitudes are often someone's farthest neighbor and end up with few or none. With μ > 0 the random ties carry influence across groups, so attitudes converge to consensus.
